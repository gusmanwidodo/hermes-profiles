# Risk

The part that actually determines outcomes. Entry timing matters far less than
position sizing and the ability to hold through drawdown.

## Position sizing first

Before any analysis of whether an asset is attractive, the binding question is
how much. A correct thesis with a position too large to hold through a 70%
drawdown produces a loss. A modest thesis sized correctly survives being wrong.

Practical anchors:

- **Only capital you can lose entirely.** These four assets have each drawn
  down 70–90% at least once. Assume it happens again.
- **Position size such that a 50% drawdown does not change your behaviour.** If
  it would force a sale or cost you sleep, it is too large.
- **Emergency fund first.** Crypto is the last allocation, not the first.

## DCA versus lump sum

**Dollar-cost averaging** — fixed amount at fixed intervals regardless of price.
Removes timing from the decision entirely. Mathematically slightly worse than a
well-timed lump sum, substantially better than a badly-timed one, and it is the
only approach that survives the reality that nobody times well.

**Lump sum** — historically outperforms DCA on average in rising markets,
because time in the market beats timing. The catch: the average conceals the
tail, and the tail in crypto is a 12-month 80% drawdown starting the week after
you buy.

**Honest guidance:** for anyone who is not a full-time trader, DCA is the
default. Lump sum makes sense only for capital that can sit untouched for years
and an investor who genuinely does not react to drawdown.

## Taking profit

Harder than entering, and the part most people skip.

- **Decide the rule before the emotion.** "Sell 20% if it doubles" written down
  in advance beats any decision made during a rally.
- **Scale out rather than calling a top.** Selling in tranches on the way up
  guarantees you neither catch the exact peak nor miss the move entirely.
- **Rebalancing is a mechanical version of the same thing.** If crypto grows
  from 5% to 15% of net worth, trimming back to target sells high without
  requiring a market call.

## Failure modes that ruin portfolios

1. **Leverage.** Liquidation makes a correct long-term thesis worthless. A 3x
   position is liquidated by a 33% move; these assets move that much routinely.
2. **Buying because it is moving.** Chasing green candles is buying from people
   who bought lower.
3. **Panic selling the bottom.** The mirror of the same mistake, and the more
   expensive one.
4. **Concentration in the highest-beta asset.** SOL up 300% feels like skill
   during the run and like a catastrophe during the 70% retrace.
5. **Custody failure.** Exchange collapses have destroyed more capital in this
   space than bad market calls. Self-custody for anything held long term.
6. **Confusing conviction with certainty.** Strong belief is not evidence.

## Ignore, always

- Anyone guaranteeing returns
- Signal groups and paid calls
- "Financial advisor" accounts on social media
- Anything framed as urgent — urgency is a sales technique, not analysis
- Including, on the timing question specifically, this skill. It supplies data
  and frameworks. It does not know what happens next.

## When advising

Ask before answering, because these change the answer entirely:

- **Time horizon.** Months and years are different questions.
- **Existing exposure.** Someone already 40% in crypto needs a different answer
  than someone at 0%.
- **Loss tolerance.** Not what they say, but what they would actually do at
  −60%.
- **Purpose.** Retirement capital and speculation capital carry different rules.

Then present conditions and trade-offs. Never a directive.

## The tax and jurisdiction gap

Tax treatment varies by country and changes. This skill does not know the
user's tax situation and should not guess at it. Flag it as something to check
with a local professional rather than producing an answer that sounds
authoritative and is wrong.
